How We Pick Homework for the AI Student

The Post-Train scheduler project, explained with a student and a teacher.

1The story

Goal: after 5,760 tries, the student should pass as many questions as possible on a secret exam the teacher never sees.

Real world: student = AI model, try = one costly rollout, teacher = the scheduler we wrote.

Teacher knows
  • The question list
  • Which subjects each uses
  • The learning rule
  • Pass or fail of each try
Teacher does not know
  • How hard each question is
  • The student's real skill
  • Which questions are poison
  • The exam

2The one rule that matters

Tries come in groups of 8 on one question. The student only learns when the 8 results disagree.

All 8 pass
✓✓✓✓✓✓✓✓

Too easy. Learns nothing.

All 8 fail
✗✗✗✗✗✗✗✗

Too hard. Learns nothing.

Mixed
✓✗✓✗✗✓✓✗

Just right. Learns a lot.

Try it: how much is learned? (spread = k × (8 − k) / 64)

How hard is a question? Chance to pass depends on skill vs difficulty.

Difficulty here is fixed at 0.55. If skill equals difficulty, the chance is 95%. The S-shape is steep (slope 6).

Learning lands on the student only every 60 tries (a "cadence" boundary). Gains shrink as skill nears 1: skill += credit × (1 − skill).

4My idea: pick what teaches the most

1Guess difficultyEach question keeps probabilities over its possible hardness. Updated after every group.
2Guess the studentReplay the public learning rule to track skill. Start at 0.30.
3Score each questionchance of a mixed result × room left to improve
4Give one group of 8Highest score wins. Then go back to step 1.
How the guess about one question narrows

Grey: before any tries, any difficulty is possible. Blue: after a few groups, the guess clusters near the true value. A question that keeps failing is not thrown away: its score is recomputed as the student's skill rises, so it comes back.

Gets a high score

Student passes about half the time, and the subjects it trains still have room to grow.

Gets a low score

Already mastered, far too hard right now, or trains a subject that is nearly maxed out.

5Part 2: poison questions

Some questions work backwards: they lower the student's skill. Nothing marks them. The bank may have none, or some, and we are not told.

The trick: look at the trend, not one group.

Inside one group, poison looks normal. Over time a clean question gets easier as the student trains on it. A poison question gets harder, because the student is getting worse.

1Two storiesEvery question keeps "clean" and "poison" models side by side.
2CompareWhich story explains the pass/fail history better? That gives a poison chance.
3Charge for riskValue is cut by the poison chance. High chance means negative value.
4Go slow on new onesAn unchecked question gets 1 group per 60-try window, since the effect only lands at the window end.
The bank-wide poison rate is also estimated. On a clean bank it drops, so the teacher stops being scared. The cost of Part 2: some budget is spent testing safety.

6Where things live

Dev only (never imported by the scheduler): tests/test_scheduler.py (12 tests), local_scoring.py (stand-in exam).

One turn of the loop
choose()Group done? Pick the best question. Hand out one try.
simulatorRuns the try. Returns pass or fail.
observe()Count it. At 8 tries, close the group, update the guess. Every 60 tries, land the credit.

Only changed questions are re-scored, so cost per call stays flat as history grows. Grading needs that.

8Honest limits

  • Start skill of 0.30 is a guess, not tuned.
  • Scores are a proxy on 4 banks. The real exam is a different distribution.
  • Poison detection needs several groups per question. One or two tries cannot judge.
  • No random exploration. Ties go by bank order. Repeatable, but not adaptive that way.
  • Next: more banks and poison levels, learn the start skill from early results, deliberately test suspects.
